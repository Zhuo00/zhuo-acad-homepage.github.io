01 / UNDERSTANDING THE STRESS

Published · 2023

What does an antibiotic
do to a microalga?

Microalgae are primary producers in aquatic ecosystems and potential partners in wastewater treatment. Before considering them for antibiotic removal, we need to understand how antibiotics affect their growth and physiology.

We exposed Chlorella sorokiniana and Scenedesmus dimorphus to moxifloxacin (MOX). MOX inhibited growth and photosynthetic activity, increased lipid peroxidation, and activated antioxidant responses. The two species responded differently, with S. dimorphus showing greater sensitivity.

The risk assessment indicated low risk at the reported surface-water concentrations, but greater concern for hospital wastewater. The exposure experiments used higher concentrations than those typically reported in the environment, so the toxicity results need to be interpreted in that context.

The next question: can microalgae tolerate another fluoroquinolone while also removing it from the water?

02 / TOLERANCE AND REMOVAL

Published · 2022

From antibiotic exposure
to cellular uptake and transformation.

With ciprofloxacin (CIP), C. sorokiniana maintained growth under the tested conditions, even though parts of its photosynthetic system were affected. The culture also released humic-like substances associated with alleviating antibiotic stress.

CIP removal reached 83.3% at an initial concentration of 20 mg/L. Combining physiological measurements, HPLC-MS/MS and RNA sequencing suggested that CIP could enter cells and undergo biodegradation through reactions including hydroxylation, demethylation and ring cleavage. Transcriptomic evidence implicated transport-related genes and intracellular oxidoreductases, particularly cytochrome P450.

This points beyond disappearance from the water: what roles do intracellular accumulation, transformation and species-specific adaptation play?

03 / COMPARING TWO SPECIES

Published · 2024

Similar removal performance.
Different physiological responses.

Comparing C. sorokiniana and S. dimorphus under CIP exposure connected antibiotic removal with the physiology supporting tolerance. Both species showed relatively limited growth inhibition under the tested conditions. Enhanced photorespiration in S. dimorphus was associated with its response to CIP stress.

Reported CIP removal efficiencies reached 89.43% and 91.73%, respectively. Bioaccumulation and biodegradation were important contributors. Transformation products and gene-expression changes supported the involvement of enzymes such as dioxygenases, oxygenases and cytochrome P450.

These efficiencies were measured in a separate experiment from the iScience study; they are not a direct performance ranking across the two papers.
