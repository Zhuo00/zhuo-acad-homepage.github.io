01 / THE STARTING POINT

Start with the acidity.
Choose the organism to match.

Food waste contains carbon that could feed microalgae. But converting starch-rich material into a usable liquid feed raises a practical challenge: thermochemical hydrolysates can be acidic, while many commonly cultivated microalgae prefer near-neutral conditions.

THE PROCESS

An acidic sugar stream

Heat and acid break starch into smaller sugars. Incomplete hydrolysis leaves a mixture of chain lengths, rather than pure glucose.

THE BIOLOGY

An acid-loving microalga

Galdieria sulphuraria thrives at low pH, making it a promising match for this feed without an extra neutralization step.

The first question: do we really need to turn every sugar chain into glucose before cultivation?

02 / STUDY 01

Published · 2026

Less glucose.
Still, the microalga grows.

We used microwave-assisted hydrolysis to prepare sugar streams from potato starch, then cultivated G. sulphuraria in the diluted hydrolysates. Under mild hydrolysis conditions, the liquid remained rich in oligosaccharides—short sugar chains—with very little free glucose. Yet it supported substantial algal growth.

A model system, a broader goal. This study tested potato starch, not mixed food waste. It establishes a mechanistic starting point for the wider food-waste-to-microalgae research theme.
